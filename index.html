01 · The role

What does a TDE do?

A Test Development Engineer turns a product design into a way of proving, on the factory floor, that every unit is good. That means a tester (fixtures, interface boards, instruments, harnesses) plus the software that drives it, delivered to Manufacturing (MFG) and kept healthy afterwards.

◎

Design

  • Test strategy and coverage
  • Design-for-test feedback to the product team
  • Tester concept, hardware and software design
⚙

Build

  • Fabricate the fixture, interface board and tester
  • Write the test software
  • Debug and prove it with real units
⇢

Deliver and sustain

  • Hand over to MFG with training and documents
  • Support NPI builds
  • Maintain and improve in sustaining

It is not software only. A TDE does hardware design, fabricates the tester and delivers it to the manufacturing line, in addition to writing the code that runs on it.

02 · Two kinds of program

CM and JDM: where the TDE joins

Celestica programs come in two flavours. The product is different, the starting point is different, and so is the TDE's job.

CM · Contract Manufacturing

The customer designs

  • Customer owns the product design
  • TDE helps develop software or hardware to test that design
  • Starts in NPI, after design handover
  • Partners: customer engineers, TE, MFG
  • Wins on: speed, tester quality, low cost of test

JDM · Joint Development Manufacturing

We co-design with the customer

  • Celestica helps design the product
  • TDE works mainly with HPS and TE
  • Starts at the proposal for the customer
  • Proposal shows our technology, our way of working and our special capabilities
  • Wins on: early testability, reusable platforms, one team from design to line

03 · NPI to sustaining

The life of a tester

Most of the TDE's work happens in the NPI phase: getting a new product to a tester that the line can trust. After that we help sustain it for the life of the product.

What the TDE hands over

A tester is only delivered when the line can run it without you. A complete handover package contains:

  • The tester, installed and accepted at the line
  • Released software, with a version number and a checksum
  • Limits file, versioned and reviewed with TE
  • Work instruction for operators
  • Training for operators and technicians
  • Maintenance and calibration plan, with intervals
  • Spare parts list, especially wear items such as pogo pins
  • Troubleshooting guide for the common faults
  • Correlation and repeatability evidence from the build phase
  • Named contacts and a support route for the sustaining phase

05 · Deep dive

The craft behind a trusted tester

1 · Test strategy: stages and defect coverage find each defect at the cheapest stage

The goal is to catch every defect class that matters, as early as possible, within the line's cycle time. No stage sees everything.

StageSeesBlind to or limited byTypical use
AOIMissing, skewed, wrong-polarity parts, solder bridgesElectrical function, hidden joints (BGA)After reflow, every board
ICT (bed-of-nails)Shorts, opens, wrong values, missing partsNeeds test points and a fixture; checks parts, not the whole functionHigh volume
Flying probeSame as ICT, no fixtureSlowPrototypes, NPI, low volume
Boundary scan (JTAG)Opens and shorts on digital nets without physical accessNeeds JTAG-capable devices and a chain that worksDense digital boards
FCTPower rails, clocks, interfaces, firmware boot, key functionsWeak at pinpointing which part failedNearly every product
Burn-in / ESSEarly-life failures under stressLong time, floor space, costHigh-reliability products
System / finalIntegrated product, interfaces, configurationSlowFinal stage

A coverage model: PCOLA / SOQ. List the defect classes: Presence, Correctness, Orientation, Live (the part works), Alignment, plus Shorts, Opens and Quality (marginal parts). For each class, mark which stage covers it. The empty cells are your risk.

Ask for DFT early. Test points, probe access, a JTAG header, programming connectors and a sensible power-up sequence cost almost nothing in the design review. After the board is built they cost a respin.

2 · Designing and fabricating the tester hardware fixtures, interface boards, instruments
  • Contact. Pogo-pin type, force and rated life are chosen for the pad size and the number of cycles. Poor contact is the classic cause of false fails. Make pin blocks replaceable and count the cycles.
  • Mechanics. Alignment pins, clamping (manual, pneumatic or vacuum), a DUT-present sensor, easy loading for operators. Test it with real, slightly imperfect boards.
  • Interface board. Routes fixture signals to instruments and carries any support circuits (loads, level shifters, relays). Keep analog and fast signals short and well grounded.
  • Instruments. Pick accuracy much tighter than the limit it checks (a common rule of thumb is 4:1 or better), with enough channels and speed for the cycle time.
  • Safety. E-stop, interlocks on guards, current limits before power-on, ESD protection, and a clear safe state.
  • Serviceability. Label connectors, add a fixture ID so cycles and calibration can be tracked, keep spares.

Typical build flow: design, review with TE and MFG, order parts (start long-lead items early), machine and assemble, bring up, debug with golden units, document.

3 · Test software for the production line structure that survives years on the line
  • Separate sequence, limits and drivers. Limits live in a versioned data file. Drivers hide instrument details.
  • Simulate hardware. A simulated instrument lets you develop and unit-test the software anywhere, and inject faults on purpose.
  • FAIL is not ERROR. FAIL means the unit is bad. ERROR means the tester or process failed and the unit has not been judged. Mixing them up scraps good units or ships bad ones.
  • Nothing tested must never pass. An empty or skipped result is an error.
  • Always leave a safe state. Outputs off, even after an exception.
  • Log everything. Serial number, station, software and limits version, timestamps, every measurement. Local copy first, then MES.
  • Wait for conditions, not for time. Poll until the rail is stable instead of a fixed sleep. It is both faster and more reliable.
  • Release like a product. Version control, tagged releases, a checksum, a change log.

4 · Measurement quality and correlation a pass is only worth the measurement behind it
TermMeaningWhy it matters
CalibrationInstrument checked against a reference on a scheduleAn uncalibrated instrument may be wrong by more than the limit
RepeatabilitySame unit, same tester, same resultNoisy measurements cause false fails and false passes
GR&RRepeatability and reproducibility across testers, fixtures and operatorsProves the measurement system is fit to judge the tolerance
Golden unitA known-good unit kept as a referenceDaily check that the tester still reads correctly. Pair it with a known-bad unit.
CorrelationTwo testers (or two stages) agree on the same unitsLets you add a second tester or move the line without surprises
GuardbandTightening the test limit inside the spec limit by the measurement uncertaintyStops marginal units from escaping, at the price of some false fails
CpkHow many 3-sigma widths fit between the mean and the nearest limitOne number for "is this measurement capable". Many programs ask for 1.33 or more.

Set limits from measured data on real units, not from the datasheet alone, and review them with TE. See example 4 for a Cpk calculation.

5 · Yield and the numbers that matter and the retest trap
MetricMeaningUse it to
First-pass yield (FPY)Share of units that pass on the first attemptSee the real health of the process and the tester
Final yieldShare that pass eventually, after retests and repairCompare with FPY: a big gap means lots of rework
Rolled throughput yield (RTY)Product of the FPY of every stageFour stages at 95% FPY each give 0.954 ≈ 81%
Retest rateShare of units tested more than onceSpot unstable fixtures and contact problems
False-fail rateGood units that fail the testerMeasure the tester's own contribution to loss
Test time / UPHSeconds per unit, units per hourKeep the tester from limiting the line
Escapes (DPPM)Bad units that reached the customer, per millionJudge whether coverage is enough

The retest trap. "Retest until it passes" hides a bad fixture and inflates the final yield. Track FPY, run a Pareto of failing steps, and fix the top item. You will build the yield maths in exercise 3.

6 · Writing a JDM proposal winning the work with technology and manner

In JDM the TDE, HPS and TE write a proposal for a customer who may be choosing between partners. The proposal shows what we propose, how we work and what only we can do well.

SectionThe question it answers
UnderstandingDo you understand the product, volumes, quality target, cost target and schedule?
Test strategy and technologyWhich stages and which technology will you use to reach the coverage, and why?
Our capabilityWhat do you already have: reusable tester platforms, modules, automation, local fabrication, relevant past programs?
Way of workingHow will we run the project: design reviews, reporting, communication, decision points?
ScopeWhat do you design, build and deliver, and what must the customer provide?
PlanWhat are the milestones through NPI, and when is the tester ready for the line?
CostOne-time development (NRE) versus cost of test per unit. What drives each?

Habits of a strong proposal: numbers over adjectives (test time in seconds, expected coverage in percent), evidence over claims (a photo of a similar tester, results from a past program), and honest risks. A proposal that hides a risk loses trust the moment it appears.

7 · Handover to MFG and sustaining after the tester leaves the lab
  • Acceptance criteria are agreed before the build: run-at-rate, correlation and repeatability targets, maximum false-fail rate, uptime.
  • Training is part of the delivery. Operators run it, technicians keep it alive. Both need material and practice.
  • Change control. Every change to software, limits or hardware gets a version, a reason and a reviewer. Test an ECO's effect on the tester before it reaches the line.
  • Preventive maintenance. Calibration dates, pin replacement by cycle count, golden-unit checks, spare parts stock.
  • Watch the data. A slow drift in yield or measurement is usually the tester, not the product. Catch it before the line complains.
  • Plan for the future. Instrument obsolescence, replicating testers for new lines, knowledge written down so that it survives staff changes.
8 · Shopfloor troubleshooting & 4M1E when the tester fails on the line

When false fails spike or a tester halts the production line, junior engineers often rush to edit software or widen test limits. Never widen limits to silence a failure. Follow a systematic 4M1E isolation procedure:

CategoryCommon root causesWhat to check and fix
Machine
(Tester & Fixture)		Pogo pins worn or oxidized, loose wiring, relay failure, instrument calibration driftRun the Golden Unit. Measure contact resistance (< 50 mΩ). Clean pins with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) or replace worn pin blocks.
Material
(DUT & Components)		PCB warpage, solder flux residue on test pads, new component lot / alternate vendorCheck test pad indentation under microscope. Inspect unpopulated or skewed parts. Verify if problem follows the board or the slot.
Method
(Test & Parameters)		Insufficient power settling time, limits too tight for process drift, floating inputsAdd settling delay before analog sampling. Check if rail voltage is measured before inrush current finishes. Review Cpk history.
Man
(Operator / Process)		Board loaded tilted, clamp closed improperly, ESD strap unplugged, barcode scan misreadObserve operator loading sequence. Verify mechanical guide pins and sensor interlocks. Re-train with Standard Work Instruction (SWI).

The Golden Rule of Isolation: Swap one variable at a time. If unit fails on Tester A, test it on Tester B. If it passes on Tester B, the problem is Tester A, not the product.

9 · Hardware safety & electrical golden rules protecting the operator and the DUT
  • Hardware E-Stop over Software Stop. Emergency Stop (E-Stop) buttons and fixture lid safety switches must break power physically through approved safety relays and contactors. Never rely on a software command (e.g. psu.write("OUTP OFF")) for operator safety.
  • Set Compliance (Current Limit) before Power-On. When writing instrument drivers or test sequences, always configure both voltage and current compliance limit before commanding output ON. If a shorted DUT is plugged in, current compliance prevents PCB traces from vaporizing or catching fire.
  • Discharge Residual Capacitance. High-capacitance power rails (e.g. 12V bulk input caps) hold energy after power-off. Always engage a bleeding resistor or active discharge circuit before fixture release, otherwise arcing will pit pogo pin tips and degrade contact life.
  • Verify Grounding & ESD Protection. All metallic fixture parts and chassis must be bonded to earth ground (< 1 Ω). Test stations must have monitored wrist strap jacks and continuous ESD bench mat grounding.
  • Isolation Relays for Programming & Sensitive Nets. Sensitive programming interfaces (SWD, JTAG, I2C) must be disconnected via relays or multiplexers before powering high-voltage functional stages to prevent back-powering MCU pins through internal ESD clamping diodes.

11 · Production programming and provisioning firmware, identity, keys

Many units leave the factory with something the tester wrote into them.

What is writtenExamplesTypical risks
FirmwareBIOS, BMC, FPGA bitstream, EEPROM imagesWrong version for the SKU, power lost during the write
IdentitySerial number, MAC addresses, UUIDDuplicates, typos, a unit shipped before it was provisioned
Keys and licencesCertificates, signing keys, feature licencesKeys leaking into code, logs or screenshots
Calibration and configurationSensor offsets, BIOS optionsStale values after a design change
  • Methods: in-system programming through JTAG, SWD or SPI with the fixture's pogo pins; an update through the BMC or the network after power-up; pre-programmed parts; an offline programmer for sockets.
  • Speed: programming a large flash can take minutes. Program in parallel, or on a separate station, so it is not on the line's critical path.
  • Verify: read back or check a hash, then record it. A write that was never verified is a hope, not a fact.
  • Baseline: the right image for each SKU comes from a versioned baseline, never from an operator's choice.
  • Identity: allocate serials and MACs from one central service, with a check digit and a duplicate check (example 12).
  • Secrets: keep keys in a hardware security module or a signing service. Never in the repository, the logs or the work instruction.
  • Traceability: record who programmed which version, when, on which station.

06 · Test sequencer

The test sequencer: the engine of a test station

The sequencer runs a test as an ordered list of steps, applies limits, decides the verdict and writes the record. In JDM programs the team mostly uses ISS3 for this. The ideas on this page are the same in every sequencer, so they make ISS3 faster to learn, and they tell you what to ask about it.

Responsibilities

ResponsibilityWhat it meansWhy it matters
Run order and flowRun steps in order, with loops, branches, and "stop on first fail" or "run everything"Safe ordering: no heat before the loop is full, no test before power is good
Steps with limitsEach step measures something and compares it with limitsThe limits are the definition of "good". They need review and versions
VerdictsPASS, FAIL, ERROR, SKIP per step, and one verdict per unitA tester fault must never look like a pass or like a bad unit (exercise 6)
Instrument and DUT accessDrivers for instruments, the DUT console and I/O, ideally with simulatorsSwap an instrument without touching the sequence. Develop without hardware
Parallel testingTest several units or sockets at the same timeCuts the cost of test (see Capacity and cost)
Operator interfaceScan the serial number, show progress and a clear resultThe operator must not be able to skip steps or change limits
Data and recordsEvery measurement, verdict, version and timestamp, per serial numberTraceability, yield analysis, and answers when a customer asks
ConfigurationLimits, station settings, product variants kept as dataA limit change is then a reviewed data change, not a code edit
Safe state and errorsAlways return outputs to a safe state, even after a failure or a crashProtects the DUT, the fixture and the people (exercise 7)
Development and debugRun, step through and dry-run a sequence, read logsSets how fast you can build and fix tests

Sequence, steps and data are three separate things

The sequence

  • The list of steps and their order
  • Flow rules: stop on fail, retry policy
  • Says what and when

The steps

  • Code that does one measurement or action
  • Talks to instruments through drivers
  • Says how

The data

  • Limits, settings, product variants
  • Versioned and reviewed
  • Says how good is good enough

You have already built the core of a sequencer: exercise 7 runs steps against limits, separates FAIL from ERROR, stops on fail and always powers off. A real sequencer adds parallel testing, an operator interface, logging and release management around that core.

A step in a generic style

This is a Python illustration of the idea, not the syntax of any particular sequencer. Every sequencer has its own way of writing a step, but a good step looks like this in all of them.

def step_rail_3v3(ctx):
    """One purpose: check the 3.3 V rail."""
    ctx.psu.output(True)
    value = ctx.dmm.read_stable("3V3", tol=0.005, timeout=2.0)   # wait for a condition, not for time
    return ctx.check("VDD_3V3", value)    # limits come from the versioned limits file

SEQUENCE = ["step_power_on", "step_rail_3v3", "step_boot", "step_uart_banner", "step_power_off"]

Anti-patterns to avoid

Hides problems
try:
    v = dmm.read()
except Exception:
    v = 0                    # a dead meter now reads 0 V
if v < 3.5:
    result = "PASS"          # ...and 0 V "passes"
Surfaces them
v = dmm.read()               # an exception becomes ERROR
status = check_limit(v, 3.135, 3.465)   # PASS or FAIL
return status

08 · OCP rack

The OCP rack: what is different

OCP racks are new to the team, so start here. OCP (Open Compute Project) is a community that publishes open hardware designs for data centres. Its Open Rack changes how a rack is built, powered and cooled, and with it, how a rack is tested.

⚡

Shared power

  • One power shelf feeds a DC busbar for the whole rack
  • Nodes plug into the busbar, no AC supply in each server
  • A battery shelf (BBU) rides through short outages
≋

Shared cooling

  • Fans can be shared, and liquid-cooled racks add a coolant manifold
  • Nodes mate to it with blind quick disconnects
  • A CDU moves the heat to facility water
⚙

Rack as the product

  • The customer buys a tested rack, not loose servers
  • Rack-level power, liquid and thermal tests join the node tests
  • Records are kept per rack serial number

The power path, and why 48 V

1 · Facility feedAC (or high-voltage DC) arrives at the rack.
2 · Power shelfRedundant rectifier modules convert it to 48 V DC.
3 · BusbarA copper bar distributes DC to every shelf.
4 · NodeOn-board converters step 48 V down to the voltages the chips need.

The BBU sits on the same busbar and bridges outages. The numbers: a 30 kW rack at 48 V carries about 625 A. At 12 V it would be about 2,500 A. Four times less current means sixteen times less resistive loss in the same copper, which is why Open Rack V3 moved to 48 V. It is still a lot of current, so contact resistance, heating and safe hot-plugging are real test topics.

From the facility to the chip

Every conversion step wastes some energy as heat, so an Open Rack keeps the chain short: one shared AC (or HVDC) to 48 V DC conversion in the power shelf, then on-board converters in each node.

Two worked examples

Is N+1 really protecting the rack?

  • Six 5.5 kW modules = 33 kW installed.
  • Lose one: 27.5 kW remain.
  • A 30 kW load now exceeds capacity, so the shelf is overloaded and nodes drop or are power-capped.
  • Fix: add a seventh module (38.5 kW, 33 kW after a failure) or cap the load. You will code this check in exercise 11.

How big a battery for 2 minutes?

  • 30 kW for 2 minutes = 30 × 2 / 60 = 1 kWh.
  • If only 80% of the battery is usable, nominal energy is 1 / 0.8 = 1.25 kWh.
  • Runtime scales inversely with load: at 15 kW the same battery lasts about 4 minutes.
  • These are illustration numbers. The real ride-through requirement comes from the program.

Power tests for a rack

  • Efficiency at several load points, measured with calibrated meters
  • Redundancy: pull one module under load. The rack must stay up
  • Hot-plug: insert a node under load, record the inrush, check neighbours for dips
  • Power cap: overload the shelf and check that nodes throttle as designed
  • BBU: charge, discharge under load, measure ride-through and the switch-over transient
  • Busbar: temperature rise at maximum current (thermal camera), contact resistance
  • Telemetry: compare reported voltage and current with an external meter
  • Protections: over-current, over-voltage and over-temperature trip as designed

An OCP rack combines a lot of weight, a lot of current and, with liquid cooling, a lot of water. Safety rules from the program and from EHS always come first. This page lists the hazards a TDE should recognise.

What OCP is

OCP is an open community where large operators, vendors and manufacturers publish hardware designs and specifications, so data-centre equipment can be built to shared designs instead of one-off ones. Work is organised in project areas, for example Rack and Power, Server, Networking, Advanced Cooling Solutions, Hardware Management and Data Center Facilities.

You may hear OCP Accepted (a product that meets an OCP specification and passed review) and OCP Inspired (based on OCP designs but not fully conformant). Ask which one the program is, and whether the customer adds its own variant on top.

09 · Cooling

Cooling: fan, water or immersion?

Every watt a rack uses becomes heat that has to go somewhere. As chips get hotter, air runs out of capacity and liquid takes over. This page compares the options, then goes deep on water cooling, the one an OCP rack is most likely to use.

Why liquid? Heat per volume

Heat removed = mass flow × specific heat × temperature rise. Water holds about 3,500 times more heat than the same volume of air, so a thin pipe does the work of a huge airflow. Try it:

Key ideas for water cooling

1 · Heat balance

  • Heat = flow × density × cp × ΔT.
  • About 1.4 L/min of water carries 1 kW at a 10 K rise. Halve the temperature rise and you need double the flow.
  • Comparing the electrical load with the heat measured in the coolant is a powerful check.

2 · Coolant choice

  • Water carries the most heat but needs corrosion and microbe control.
  • PG25 (25% propylene glycol with inhibitors) protects against corrosion and freezing, but carries a little less heat (about 6% more flow with this course's constants) and is more viscous, so ΔP rises.
  • Use the coolant the design specifies. Never top up with something else.

3 · Materials and water quality

  • Every wetted material must be compatible: copper, brass, stainless steel, aluminium, seals, plastics.
  • Mixing metals such as aluminium and copper in a water loop invites galvanic corrosion.
  • Control particles (filters), conductivity, pH and microbes. Follow the vendor's approved list.

4 · Pressure and flow

  • ΔP adds up across cold plates, quick disconnects and the manifold.
  • The pump delivers whatever flow makes its curve meet the loop's resistance curve.
  • Nodes should receive nearly equal flow (hydraulic balance). A clogged or kinked part shows up as low flow or high ΔP.

5 · Dew point and condensation

  • If coolant is colder than the room air's dew point, water condenses on pipes and cold plates and can drip on electronics.
  • Keep the supply temperature above the dew point with a margin, and watch humidity.

6 · Air in the loop

  • Bubbles block narrow channels, make noise and cause hot spots.
  • Fill from the lowest point, vent the highest points, circulate until flow and ΔP are stable.

7 · Leaks and detection

  • Layers: leak-tight connectors, drip trays, rope or point sensors, and flow or pressure anomalies.
  • A policy decides the response: alarm, throttle, shut down, close isolation valves.
  • Prove it with fault injection, not just by reading the sensor.

8 · Safety and handling

  • Water and electricity share the rack: keep wet and electrical zones apart and grounded.
  • Read the safety data sheets for glycol and inhibitors. Keep a spill kit at the station.
  • Dispose of coolant according to the site rules.

A node is "right" only compared with something. The golden configuration is that something: the expected firmware versions, hardware inventory and settings for one SKU, kept in a versioned file.

What it contains

  • BIOS, BMC, NIC, drive and accelerator firmware versions
  • CPU model and count, DIMM count, size and speed
  • Drives, NICs, GPUs and PCIe link widths and speeds
  • BIOS settings and boot order
  • The test limits version it goes with

How it is used

  • Read the actual values from the BMC and the operating system
  • Compare them with the baseline for this SKU (exercise 13)
  • Report every difference, with the path and both values
  • Fail on a mismatch. Never silently "fix" and pass

Keeping it trustworthy

  • One baseline per SKU, chosen by the serial number or the route, not by the operator.
  • Changes only through change control. A new firmware release means an ECO, a new baseline version and a record of why.
  • Review the baseline with TE and the customer. A wrong baseline passes every unit that is wrong in the same way.
  • Record what you found, not only the verdict, so a later question can be answered from the data.
  • Identity is separate. Serial numbers and MACs differ per unit, so they are checked for format and uniqueness (example 12), not for equality.

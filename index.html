Overview

What does a TDE do?

A Test Development Engineer turns a product design into a way of proving, on the factory floor, that every unit is good. That means a tester (fixtures, interface boards, instruments, harnesses) plus the software that drives it, delivered to Manufacturing (MFG) and kept healthy afterwards.

◎

Design

  • Test strategy and coverage
  • Design-for-test feedback to the product team
  • Tester concept, hardware and software design
⚙

Build

  • Fabricate the fixture, interface board and tester
  • Write the test software
  • Debug and prove it with real units
⇢

Deliver and sustain

  • Hand over to MFG with training and documents
  • Support NPI builds
  • Maintain and improve in sustaining

It is not software only. A TDE does hardware design, fabricates the tester and delivers it to the manufacturing line, in addition to writing the code that runs on it.

CM, JDM and the life of a tester

Celestica programs come in two flavours. The product is different, the starting point is different, and so is the TDE's job. Both end in the same life of a tester, from proposal to sustaining.

CM · Contract Manufacturing

The customer designs

  • Customer owns the product design
  • TDE helps develop software or hardware to test that design
  • Starts in NPI, after design handover
  • Partners: customer engineers, TE, MFG
  • Wins on: speed, tester quality, low cost of test

JDM · Joint Development Manufacturing

We co-design with the customer

  • Celestica helps design the product
  • TDE works mainly with HPS and TE
  • Starts at the proposal for the customer
  • Proposal shows our technology, our way of working and our special capabilities
  • Wins on: early testability, reusable platforms, one team from design to line

Test stages on the production line

Each stage can see some defects and is blind to others. A good test strategy puts each check at the cheapest stage that can see the defect. Which stages exist depends on the product and the customer.

Anatomy of a production tester

Software on top, hardware below, the product at the bottom. Each layer talks only to its neighbour, which is what lets you simulate, swap and maintain parts independently.

Session 1 · Project management

From RFQ to purchase order

Most test projects start with a Request for Quotation (RFQ): the customer describes what they need and asks for a solution, a price and a date. In this phase the TDE has to understand the request, design a credible solution quickly, estimate it honestly and help the team win an order that can actually be delivered. This session follows one project from the RFQ to handover.

Ten steps, in two halves. The first half wins the work and ends with a purchase order (PO). The second half delivers it and ends when Manufacturing owns the tester. Click any step for what the TDE does there.

Why a TDE needs this. The price and the date are decided in the first half, before any hardware exists. A TDE who skips the questions, guesses the effort or forgets the spares makes a promise the second half cannot keep. Most painful projects were lost at the quotation, not at the bench.

Titles differ between sites and programs. On a small CM job the TDE may do almost all of this alone. On a large JDM program there is a program manager and a sales or business development team. What matters is that each row has exactly one A (accountable, the one who answers for it).

Good questions are

  • Specific: "peak units per day" rather than "volume?"
  • Closed where possible, so the answer is one number or yes/no
  • Numbered, so the answer can say "Q3: yes"
  • Explained: one line on why it changes the offer
  • Asked once, through one channel, usually the program manager or sales

Good assumptions are

  • Testable: a number, a date, a document revision
  • Tied to price: "changes are quoted separately" when it matters
  • Visible: in the proposal and in the quotation, not only in an email
  • Few and important: ten real ones beat fifty legal ones
  • Reviewed when the customer answers. Re-issue the offer if they change the price

Management decides whether to bid. The TDE answers one question for them: can we deliver this, technically and on time, and what would stop us? Answer in writing, in one page.

  • The required coverage is reachable with the access the design gives
  • The cycle time fits the takt time with a realistic number of stations
  • Instrument and fixture lead times fit the schedule
  • We have, or can borrow, the skills and the hours
  • An existing platform or module can be reused
  • Safety and standards requirements are understood
  • Customer-supplied items have dates that work
  • The risks we cannot control are named

Go

All items are clear. Quote normally.

Go with conditions

Some items depend on the customer. Quote, and write them as assumptions or options.

No-go, or not yet

A requirement cannot be met as written. Say why and what would make it possible (for example a longer lead time or a design change).

Checklist before it goes out

  • Every RFQ requirement appears in the compliance matrix
  • The key numbers are the same in the summary, the body and the quotation
  • Each number has a source: measured, calculated or assumed
  • Assumptions and exclusions match the quotation word for word
  • Customer-supplied items have dates
  • Acceptance criteria are measurable
  • Risks are honest and each has an action
  • No other customer's name, data or photo without permission
  • Reviewed by TE and a second TDE
  • Document number, revision and date on every page. Sent as PDF

Presenting it to the customer

Ask for a review call after sending. Thirty minutes is enough if you keep to a story:

  1. What you asked for (2 min): restate the need and the key requirements. Ask "did we get this right?"
  2. What we propose (8 min): strategy and the station in one diagram, the key numbers.
  3. Why it will work (5 min): evidence from a similar tester, the measurement capability, the reuse.
  4. The plan (5 min): milestones, what we need from you and when.
  5. Risks and open points (5 min): the exceptions, the assumptions that matter most.
  6. Next step (5 min): questions, and what decision is needed by when.

After the call, send the minutes within a day: questions asked, answers given, actions with an owner and a date. If an answer changes the scope or the price, issue a new revision of the proposal and the quotation together.

How many testers does the line need?

The line must output one unit every takt time (available time divided by demand). Each tester supplies only part of its day, because of uptime, and retests repeat work. A multi-up fixture tests several units per cycle.

seconds needed per day = demand x cycle x (1 + retest rate) / units per cycle
seconds one tester gives = line seconds per day x uptime
testers = round up ( needed / one tester gives )

Testing connections without probes.

Boundary scan (IEEE 1149.1, usually called JTAG) puts a small cell at each pin of a compliant chip. Through a 4 or 5 pin Test Access Port (TCK, TMS, TDI, TDO and optionally TRST) the tester can drive and read those pins, even where it cannot touch the board.

What it finds

  • Opens and shorts on nets between scan-capable devices
  • Stuck nets
  • With cluster tests, connections to simple non-scan parts
  • Programming of flash and other devices through the same port

What it does not

  • Component values (resistors, capacitors)
  • Analog behaviour
  • Anything on devices that have no scan cells
  • Timing at full speed, in general
  1. Check the chain first. Read each device's ID code and compare it with what the BSDL file expects. A broken chain makes every later result meaningless.
  2. Run the interconnect test. The tool drives patterns and reports the failing nets and pins by name.
  3. Run cluster and memory tests where they apply.
  4. Program devices, if the program uses JTAG for that, and verify by reading back.

You need: a BSDL file for every scan device, the netlist, an intact chain from TDI to TDO, and access to the TAP signals (a header, test pads or a connector). Ask these in the design review:

  • Are all devices with a TAP in one chain, and in which order?
  • Which devices have no BSDL file?
  • Are TAP signals accessible, and buffered if the chain is long?
  • Is anything holding the chain in reset or disabling it at test time?
  • Which nets does the tool report as covered, and which are not?
  • How long does a full run take, and can programming share the connection?

Tester hardware and fixtures

The physical tester: instruments that measure accurately enough, a fixture that contacts the product thousands of times without false fails, an interface board between them, and a safety concept that protects the operator and the product. Most false fails start where the probe meets the pad.

Fixtures, interface boards, instruments.

  • Contact. Pogo-pin type, force and rated life are chosen for the pad size and the number of cycles. Poor contact is the classic cause of false fails. Make pin blocks replaceable and count the cycles.
  • Mechanics. Alignment pins, clamping (manual, pneumatic or vacuum), a DUT-present sensor, easy loading for operators. Test it with real, slightly imperfect boards.
  • Interface board. Routes fixture signals to instruments and carries any support circuits (loads, level shifters, relays). Keep analog and fast signals short and well grounded.
  • Instruments. Pick accuracy much tighter than the limit it checks (a common rule of thumb is 4:1 or better), with enough channels and speed for the cycle time.
  • Safety. E-stop, interlocks on guards, current limits before power-on, ESD protection, and a clear safe state.
  • Serviceability. Label connectors, add a fixture ID so cycles and calibration can be tracked, keep spares.

Typical build flow: design, review with TE and MFG, order parts (start long-lead items early), machine and assemble, bring up, debug with golden units, document.

Typical spring-probe forces are roughly 0.5 to 2 N at working travel, so read the datasheet. Atmospheric pressure is about 101 kPa, so a vacuum can never exceed that, and practical fixtures deliver only part of it.

Protecting the operator and the DUT.

  • Hardware E-Stop over Software Stop. Emergency Stop (E-Stop) buttons and fixture lid safety switches must break power physically through approved safety relays and contactors. Never rely on a software command (e.g. psu.write("OUTP OFF")) for operator safety.
  • Set Compliance (Current Limit) before Power-On. When writing instrument drivers or test sequences, always configure both voltage and current compliance limit before commanding output ON. If a shorted DUT is plugged in, current compliance prevents PCB traces from vaporizing or catching fire.
  • Discharge Residual Capacitance. High-capacitance power rails (e.g. 12V bulk input caps) hold energy after power-off. Always engage a bleeding resistor or active discharge circuit before fixture release, otherwise arcing will pit pogo pin tips and degrade contact life.
  • Verify Grounding & ESD Protection. All metallic fixture parts and chassis must be bonded to earth ground (< 1 Ω). Test stations must have monitored wrist strap jacks and continuous ESD bench mat grounding.
  • Isolation Relays for Programming & Sensitive Nets. Sensitive programming interfaces (SWD, JTAG, I2C) must be disconnected via relays or multiplexers before powering high-voltage functional stages to prevent back-powering MCU pins through internal ESD clamping diodes.

The retest trap. "Retest until it passes" hides a bad fixture and inflates the final yield. Track FPY, run a Pareto of failing steps, and fix the top item. You will build the yield maths in exercise 3.

Never "retest until it passes".

When a unit fails, what happens next decides whether the data stays honest. A written policy stops people from retesting until something passes.

  1. Failure. The tester records FAIL (or ERROR) with the step and the measurement.
  2. One controlled retest, after a defined action (reseat the unit, check the fixture). The retest is recorded as attempt 2, never overwritten.
  3. Pass on the retest? The first-pass result still counts as a fail in FPY. Log it as a possible fixture or contact issue (NTF) and watch the trend.
  4. Fail again? Treat it as a real defect. Send it to repair with the failure data.
  5. Repair. Record the defect code, location and cause. After repair, run the full test again, not only the step that failed.
  6. Limit the loops. A unit that needs more than a set number of repairs or rework cycles is scrapped or escalated, because every thermal cycle can damage it.
  7. Feed it back. A weekly Pareto of defect codes goes to the process team and the design team.

Rules for good wiring drawings

  • Every wire has a unique ID, printed on a label at both ends. The label, the drawing and the wire list use the same ID.
  • Protect every circuit with a breaker or fuse sized for the wire, not only for the load.
  • One protective-earth route: every conductive part is bonded to a single earth bar, and the bar to the supply earth.
  • Separate mains, DC power and sensitive signals. Cross them at right angles where they must meet.
  • Measurement grounding is decided on the drawing (for example a single reference point), and shields are terminated as drawn.
  • Leave spare terminals (roughly a fifth) and spare cable length for changes and repairs.
  • Revision control: a revision number, date, author and reviewer on every sheet. Changes during the build are red-lined and become the as-built drawing.
  • Mains connections are designed and made according to the local electrical code, by a qualified electrician.

The power path, and why 48 V

1 · Facility feedAC (or high-voltage DC) arrives at the rack.
2 · Power shelfRedundant rectifier modules convert it to 48 V DC.
3 · BusbarA copper bar distributes DC to every shelf.
4 · NodeOn-board converters step 48 V down to the voltages the chips need.

The BBU sits on the same busbar and bridges outages. The numbers: a 30 kW rack at 48 V carries about 625 A. At 12 V it would be about 2,500 A. Four times less current means sixteen times less resistive loss in the same copper, which is why Open Rack V3 moved to 48 V. It is still a lot of current, so contact resistance, heating and safe hot-plugging are real test topics.

From the facility to the chip

Every conversion step wastes some energy as heat, so an Open Rack keeps the chain short: one shared AC (or HVDC) to 48 V DC conversion in the power shelf, then on-board converters in each node.

Two worked examples

Is N+1 really protecting the rack?

  • Six 5.5 kW modules = 33 kW installed.
  • Lose one: 27.5 kW remain.
  • A 30 kW load now exceeds capacity, so the shelf is overloaded and nodes drop or are power-capped.
  • Fix: add a seventh module (38.5 kW, 33 kW after a failure) or cap the load. You will code this check in exercise 11.

How big a battery for 2 minutes?

  • 30 kW for 2 minutes = 30 × 2 / 60 = 1 kWh.
  • If only 80% of the battery is usable, nominal energy is 1 / 0.8 = 1.25 kWh.
  • Runtime scales inversely with load: at 15 kW the same battery lasts about 4 minutes.
  • These are illustration numbers. The real ride-through requirement comes from the program.

Power tests for a rack

  • Efficiency at several load points, measured with calibrated meters
  • Redundancy: pull one module under load. The rack must stay up
  • Hot-plug: insert a node under load, record the inrush, check neighbours for dips
  • Power cap: overload the shelf and check that nodes throttle as designed
  • BBU: charge, discharge under load, measure ride-through and the switch-over transient
  • Busbar: temperature rise at maximum current (thermal camera), contact resistance
  • Telemetry: compare reported voltage and current with an external meter
  • Protections: over-current, over-voltage and over-temperature trip as designed

Every watt a rack uses becomes heat that has to go somewhere. As chips get hotter, air runs out of capacity and liquid takes over. This page compares the options, then goes deep on water cooling, the one an OCP rack is most likely to use.

Key ideas for water cooling

1 · Heat balance

  • Heat = flow × density × cp × ΔT.
  • About 1.4 L/min of water carries 1 kW at a 10 K rise. Halve the temperature rise and you need double the flow.
  • Comparing the electrical load with the heat measured in the coolant is a powerful check.

2 · Coolant choice

  • Water carries the most heat but needs corrosion and microbe control.
  • PG25 (25% propylene glycol with inhibitors) protects against corrosion and freezing, but carries a little less heat (about 6% more flow with this course's constants) and is more viscous, so ΔP rises.
  • Use the coolant the design specifies. Never top up with something else.

3 · Materials and water quality

  • Every wetted material must be compatible: copper, brass, stainless steel, aluminium, seals, plastics.
  • Mixing metals such as aluminium and copper in a water loop invites galvanic corrosion.
  • Control particles (filters), conductivity, pH and microbes. Follow the vendor's approved list.

4 · Pressure and flow

  • ΔP adds up across cold plates, quick disconnects and the manifold.
  • The pump delivers whatever flow makes its curve meet the loop's resistance curve.
  • Nodes should receive nearly equal flow (hydraulic balance). A clogged or kinked part shows up as low flow or high ΔP.

5 · Dew point and condensation

  • If coolant is colder than the room air's dew point, water condenses on pipes and cold plates and can drip on electronics.
  • Keep the supply temperature above the dew point with a margin, and watch humidity.

6 · Air in the loop

  • Bubbles block narrow channels, make noise and cause hot spots.
  • Fill from the lowest point, vent the highest points, circulate until flow and ΔP are stable.

7 · Leaks and detection

  • Layers: leak-tight connectors, drip trays, rope or point sensors, and flow or pressure anomalies.
  • A policy decides the response: alarm, throttle, shut down, close isolation valves.
  • Prove it with fault injection, not just by reading the sensor.

8 · Safety and handling

  • Water and electricity share the rack: keep wet and electrical zones apart and grounded.
  • Read the safety data sheets for glycol and inhibitors. Keep a spill kit at the station.
  • Dispose of coolant according to the site rules.

A node is "right" only compared with something. The golden configuration is that something: the expected firmware versions, hardware inventory and settings for one SKU, kept in a versioned file.

What it contains

  • BIOS, BMC, NIC, drive and accelerator firmware versions
  • CPU model and count, DIMM count, size and speed
  • Drives, NICs, GPUs and PCIe link widths and speeds
  • BIOS settings and boot order
  • The test limits version it goes with

How it is used

  • Read the actual values from the BMC and the operating system
  • Compare them with the baseline for this SKU (exercise 13)

  • Fail on a mismatch. Never silently "fix" and pass

  • Identity is separate. Serial numbers and MACs differ per unit, so they are checked for format and uniqueness (example 12), not for equality.

Test software architecture

Test software runs on the line for years, on many stations, operated by people who never saw the code. It must give honest verdicts, leave the hardware safe, record everything, and be changed only in a controlled way. This page covers the structure that makes that possible. The pages after it go into the sequencer and into Python itself.

Structure that survives years on the line.

  • Separate sequence, limits and drivers. Limits live in a versioned data file. Drivers hide instrument details.
  • Simulate hardware. A simulated instrument lets you develop and unit-test the software anywhere, and inject faults on purpose.
  • FAIL is not ERROR. FAIL means the unit is bad. ERROR means the tester or process failed and the unit has not been judged. Mixing them up scraps good units or ships bad ones.
  • Nothing tested must never pass. An empty or skipped result is an error.
  • Always leave a safe state. Outputs off, even after an exception.
  • Log everything. Serial number, station, software and limits version, timestamps, every measurement. Local copy first, then MES.
  • Wait for conditions, not for time. Poll until the rail is stable instead of a fixed sleep. It is both faster and more reliable.
  • Release like a product. Version control, tagged releases, a checksum, a change log.

Test software is released like a product, because a wrong version on the line ships bad units or scraps good ones. Limits files and sequences are part of the release, not something edited on the station.

While developing

  • Everything in version control: code, sequences, limits, configuration, drivers
  • A branch per change, reviewed by a second engineer before merge
  • Unit tests against simulators run on every change
  • No secrets in the repository: keys and passwords come from a vault or the station

When releasing

  • A version number and a tag in version control
  • A checksum of the released package, checked by the station at start
  • Release notes: changes, reason, known issues, how to roll back
  • Verified on a station with golden and known-bad units
  • Approved by TE (and the customer where required)

Release checklist

  • Change request approved and linked
  • Code reviewed and merged
  • All unit tests pass
  • Limits file reviewed with TE
  • Golden unit passes, known-bad unit fails at the right step
  • Fault injection gives ERROR, never PASS
  • Version and checksum recorded
  • Release notes written
  • Old version kept for rollback
  • Operators and technicians told what changed

The misconception

In many languages a variable is a box that holds a value, and b = a copies the value into a second box. In Python a variable is a name attached to an object. Assignment attaches a name. It never copies.

a = [1, 2, 3]
b = a              # b is a second NAME for the same list
b.append(4)
print(a)           # [1, 2, 3, 4]   a changed too
print(a is b)      # True: one object, two names
c = a.copy()       # a NEW list with the same items
print(c is a)      # False

What people believe, and what Python really does

BeliefWhat Python really doesSee
b = a makes a copyIt makes another name for the same objectExample 1
My function gets its own copy of the listIt gets the same object. Changing it inside changes it for the caller. Rebinding the name inside does notExample 3
copy() copies everythingA shallow copy: a new outer container, the same inner objects. Use copy.deepcopy for independenceExample 2
[[0] * 3] * 3 makes a 3 by 3 gridIt repeats one inner list three timesExample 2
== and is mean the same== compares values. is compares identity (the same object)Example 1
Default arguments are created on every callThey are evaluated once, when def runsExample 3
+= always builds a new objectFor a list it changes the object in place. For numbers and strings it builds a new oneExample 1

Rules of thumb

  • Do not change an argument unless the function is documented to do so. Return a new object instead.
  • Copy when you need independence: list(x) or x.copy() for a flat container, copy.deepcopy(x) when there are containers inside.
  • Use None as the default for a list or dict, and create it inside the function.
  • Use is only for None, True, False and deliberate sentinel objects. Never for numbers or strings.
  • Build grids with a comprehension: [[0] * 3 for _ in range(3)].

The misconception

People call __init__ "the constructor", and expect it to work like a constructor in C++ or Java. In Python __new__ creates the object and __init__ then initialises it. In practice you write only __init__, but the difference explains several surprises.

class Sensor:
    def __init__(self, name, unit="V"):
        self.name = name           # an attribute of THIS object
        self.unit = unit
        self.readings = []         # created per object

    @classmethod
    def from_text(cls, text):      # an alternative constructor
        name, unit = text.split(":")
        return cls(name, unit)

s = Sensor.from_text("vdd:V")

What people believe, and what Python really does

BeliefWhat Python really doesSee
__init__ creates the object__new__ creates it, then __init__ sets it upExample 4
__init__ must return the objectIt must return NoneExample 4
I can write several __init__ with different argumentsThere is no overloading: the last definition replaces the earlier ones. Use default arguments or a @classmethodExample 4
self is a keywordIt is only the conventional name of the first parameter, which receives the object
Writing name = name in __init__ sets the attributeIt creates a local variable and throws it away. You need self.name = name
A list written in the class body belongs to each objectIt is one list shared by all objectsExample 7

Rules of thumb

  • Set every attribute in __init__, so all objects have the same shape.
  • Create mutable per-object state (lists, dicts) inside __init__.
  • For a second way of building an object, write a @classmethod that returns cls(...).
  • Keep __init__ cheap. In test software, do not open an instrument in __init__. Build the object, then call connect(), so tests and simulators can create objects without hardware.
  • Give classes a useful __repr__, so failures print something readable.

The misconception

A subclass gets everything from its parent, so the parent's setup must run automatically. It does not, if the subclass defines its own __init__. Then you must call the parent's with super().__init__(...).

class Instrument:
    def __init__(self, name):
        self.name = name
        self.connected = False

class Dmm(Instrument):
    def __init__(self, name, digits=6):
        super().__init__(name)     # without this, name and connected never exist
        self.digits = digits

    def describe(self):            # override and extend
        return super().describe() + f", {self.digits} digits"

What people believe, and what Python really does

BeliefWhat Python really doesSee
The parent's __init__ always runsOnly when the subclass has no __init__ of its own. Otherwise call super().__init__()Example 5
super() means my parent classIt means the next class in the method resolution order (MRO)Example 6
Overriding a method changes it for the parent tooOnly objects of the subclass use the new versionExample 5
With multiple parents, Python picks one at randomThe order is fixed, left to right, and shown by Class.__mro__Example 6
Inheritance is the way to reuse codeUse it for "is a". For "has a", store an object in an attribute (composition)

Inherit or compose?

Inherit: "is a"

  • A Dmm is an Instrument
  • It can be used wherever an Instrument is expected
  • It reuses and extends the parent's behaviour

Compose: "has a"

  • A TestStation has instruments
  • Store them in attributes and call their methods
  • Easier to change and to test with simulators

Rules of thumb

  • Call super().__init__(...) first in a subclass __init__.
  • Extend the parent's method with super().method() instead of copying its code.
  • Keep hierarchies shallow. Prefer composition when in doubt.
  • Let each class answer the same method its own way (polymorphism) instead of testing the type with isinstance.

Public, _protected and __private

Python has no public, protected or private keywords. It relies on naming conventions and trust ("we are all consenting adults").

  • Use class variables for constants and shared settings (immutable values, written in UPPER_CASE for constants).
  • Create per-object state in __init__, and never put a list or dict in the class body unless you want it shared.
  • Change class-level state through the class name: Station.count += 1.
  • Prefer a leading single underscore for internal names. Use double underscores only to avoid clashes in subclasses.
  • Use @property instead of Java-style getters and setters.

Why a driver base class

Test steps should depend on a contract ("measure a voltage on a channel"), not on an instrument model. An abstract base class (ABC) writes the contract down. Every real driver, every other model and the simulator implement it, so one sequence runs against any of them.

from abc import ABC, abstractmethod

class DmmDriver(ABC):
    CHANNELS = ("CH1", "CH2")

    def measure_voltage(self, channel):          # the public contract: written ONCE
        self._check_open()
        if channel not in self.CHANNELS:
            raise ValueError(f"unknown channel {channel!r}")
        return float(self._query(f"MEAS:VOLT? {channel}"))

    @abstractmethod
    def _query(self, command):                   # each implementation provides only this
        ...

class ScpiDmm(DmmDriver):
    def __init__(self, resource):                # the connection is passed IN
        self._resource = resource                # a PyVISA resource, or a fake in tests
    def _query(self, command):
        return self._resource.query(command)

What Big O says

Big O describes how the work grows as the input size n grows, ignoring constant factors. It answers "what happens when the data gets ten times bigger?", not "how many milliseconds is this?".

The O(1) of sets and dicts is the average case and needs hashable keys (see the notes on __hash__ above). Memory grows too: a set or dict costs space to save time.

Count the work, not the seconds

A stopwatch changes from run to run, and tells you about one machine. Counting operations (comparisons, instrument queries) gives the same answer every time, and shows the shape of the growth. The examples and the exercise on this site count comparisons with a small helper class.

Where it matters in test software

  • Duplicate serial numbers in a lot of 100,000 units: a list check is about 5,000,000,000 comparisons, a set is about 100,000.
  • Looking up each step's limits by scanning a list for every step: O(steps²). Build a dict once: O(steps).
  • A big log file: build an index once instead of scanning it for every question.
  • "N+1" instrument queries: ask once for all channels, not once per channel in a loop. The slow part is the instrument, not Python.

When it does not matter

  • A sequence has tens of steps, not millions. A list of five step names is fine, and clearer than a set.
  • In a station, the cost is nearly always waiting: a rail that takes two seconds to settle outweighs any Python loop (see Capacity and cost).
  • Readability first. Measure with timeit or a profiler before you optimise anything.
  • Analysis of thousands of units, large logs and data pipelines are where it does start to matter.
